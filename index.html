Cover story · EdTech · May 2025 – June 2026

Ten people, four months, one hundred lessons

BilimTube is a video-learning platform for children aged four to thirteen. As technical lead I took it from an empty repository to launch, then to its first hundred users.

Role: technical lead · Winner, Dive into Silicon Valley

  • 10people on the team
  • 4months to launch
  • 100+short video lessons
  • ~100parents and kids interviewed
  • 100first users

The brief was simple to say and hard to do: short, safe lessons for three age bands, a player children could use without help, and progress their parents could see. We were ten people, most of us students, and we had four months.

Before writing much code I sat with roughly a hundred parents and children and watched where they got stuck. The findings were unglamorous and decisive. Kids could not find lessons for their age, so age filtering moved to the front. They abandoned long videos, so lessons got shorter. Parents navigated differently from children, so navigation was reworked around both.

I built the backend in Django and PostgreSQL: the schema and REST APIs for courses, progress tracking and answer submission, with Redis holding session state. On the frontend, in React and TypeScript, I wrote the student dashboard, the course player, favorites and the authentication flow.

Malformed model output never reached the database.

Language models generate lessons and grade children's free-form answers. Models occasionally return something that is not quite what was asked for, so every response passes schema validation and is retried when it fails. Nothing malformed is stored, which matters a great deal when the reader is seven.

We shipped in Docker through GitLab CI/CD, with pytest and Playwright covering the critical paths, and won Dive into Silicon Valley along the way.

Climate · Cal Hacks 13.0 · San Francisco, Oct 23–25, 2026

Wells the maps forgot

Abandoned oil and gas wells leak methane, and many were never recorded. Dowser reads 1950s survey maps to find candidates, ranks them for plugging crews and checks them in the field with a methane sensor. I owned the machine-learning pipeline.

Team project · My part: data preparation, labelling, detector training and evaluation

Old United States Geological Survey quadrangles mark oil wells with a small symbol. Many of those wells were later abandoned, and some never made it into today's state registry. If a detector can find the symbols, every symbol without a matching documented well is a lead worth checking.

We started with Kern County, California: the 1954 Oil Center and Oildale sheets and the 1992 Oil Center edition, all 1:24,000 GeoTIFFs. I cut them into 1024-pixel tiles with about 20 percent overlap, kept each tile's coordinate system so a detection maps back to latitude and longitude, and split the area geographically into 84 training, 13 validation and 30 test images, so the model is never graded on the neighborhood it learned from.

The detector is a fine-tuned YOLO11n. The first model learned from 568 hand-labelled boxes and reached an F1 of 0.60 on validation. Adding 49 reviewed labels raised it to 0.80, with recall of 0.90. Each training run took under a minute on a laptop.

Detections are candidates, not verified wells.

Those are validation numbers, not a final test, and the honest caveats stay attached: the confident positives come from one era of maps, and digits, tanks and rectangular signs still fool the model. That is why the app ranks candidates for people to check rather than declaring anything found.

Games · Hackathon theme: Impractical Inventions

The engine nobody taught chess

Every turn, each piece is handed a random way of moving. You win by capturing a secret piece that neither player can see. The opponent was never told any of the rules, and has to learn them while it plays you.

Plain JavaScript modules · no dependencies · every claim reproducible with one test command

Normal engines are given the rules and then search. Here the rules change every turn, so there is a real learning problem. The agent sees a label (“this piece moves as a knight”) and has to work out what the label means, what wins the game, and which of your sixteen pieces is the secret one.

The trick that makes it learn within a single game is symmetry. A knight's jump to the upper right and its jump to the lower left are the same fact rotated, so 225 possible displacements collapse into 36 groups under the symmetries of the square. One observed jump teaches all eight.

To guess your secret piece it watches what you protect. People shield what they cannot afford to lose, and every capture that does not end the game rules a piece out.

With no budget for questions, the agent never gets past 50 percent.

Each turn it may ask a few yes-or-no questions (“may I move this there?”) and it spends them where it is least certain. Watching legal moves alone never teaches it what is forbidden; it has to try illegal moves to learn that they are illegal.

Business · Bishkek · 2026

Six coffees, one card

Paper loyalty cards get lost, and app-based ones need a download nobody wants. Krug puts a coffee shop's stamp card into Apple Wallet and Google Wallet. It is my own startup, and it is in the stage that matters most: talking to owners.

Founder · customer discovery with coffee shops in Bishkek

The idea: a guest saves the card once, from a link or a QR code. At the counter the barista scans it and confirms the purchase, and the card fills up on the guest's phone. Each coffee shop can have its own look, from warm brown to black-and-white minimalism.

The harder questions are not technical. What stops a cashier from stamping friends' cards when there is no cash-register integration? I wrote the threat model before any of that code: per-staff sign-in, single-use operations, owner-set limits and an activity log, with the remaining risk stated plainly to owners instead of promised away.

The concept site and a four-step video walkthrough are live, with an introductory offer for the first ten coffee shops.

Dispatch · September 2026 – present

Now: an agent that adapts to the wrong answer you picked

At San José State I am an undergraduate researcher in Prof. William Andreopoulos's AI-in-education group, and the primary developer of the AI agent at the core of its assessment system.

The agent offers students realistic answer choices and adapts to the one they pick, because a well-chosen wrong option says more about what a student misunderstands than a blank. Alongside it I support the group's research on evaluating language-model output, including a literature review on retrieval-augmented generation and LLM output quality.
